Yet there is no lymph-node mass, no nodal disease, and no discrete tumor to biopsy. This pattern is characteristic of **intravascular large B-cell lymphoma**: a febrile, multisystem, marrow-infiltrating illness with strikingly elevated LDH, ferritin, and soluble IL-2 receptor; multifocal cerebral infarction and fluctuating encephalopathy with normal large vessels and negative CSF cytology; pathologic livedo and telangiectatic skin lesions; secondary hemophagocytic syndrome; and a characteristic absence of lymphadenopathy or a tissue mass. The pathophysiology accounts for the clinical findings. In intravascular large B-cell lymphoma, the neoplastic large B cells lack the surface adhesion molecules — notably β1 integrin (CD29) and ICAM-1 (CD54) — required to exit the vasculature, so they proliferate and accumulate within the lumina of small and medium vessels, particularly capillaries, venules, and arterioles, rather than forming a mass. The occlusion of cerebral microvessels produces multifocal, multi-territory infarcts of varying age with patent large arteries, encephalopathy out of proportion to any single lesion, and microhemorrhages. The same intraluminal proliferation in dermal vessels produces pathologic livedo and telangiectatic plaques. Because the malignant cells remain within vessels and seldom shed into the subarachnoid space, *CSF cytology is characteristically negative*; the negative cytology and flow cytometry therefore do not exclude this diagnosis. The systemic variant infiltrates the marrow, liver, and spleen, producing cytopenias and triggering hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, with the high LDH, ferritin, soluble IL-2 receptor, and β2-microglobulin reflecting the tumor and the inflammatory response it provokes. The reason the bone marrow aspirate was initially unrevealing is also instructive: in intravascular lymphoma the malignant cells lodge within the marrow sinusoids and are easily overlooked unless specifically sought on the core biopsy with immunostaining, and the aspirate may miss them entirely. The diagnostic test is therefore not another scan but a *tissue biopsy of an involved small-vessel bed*. The procedure with the highest yield is a **random skin biopsy** — deep punch or incisional biopsies that include the subcutaneous fat, taken from the livedoid and telangiectatic areas and even from normal-appearing skin, since the disease may be present in cutaneous vessels where the skin looks clinically unremarkable. A repeat bone marrow core biopsy with careful examination of the sinusoids, and, if the skin and marrow were unrevealing, a brain biopsy, would be alternatives. In this case the skin biopsy would be expected to establish the diagnosis. --- ## Clinical Diagnosis Fever of unknown origin with subacute multifocal encephalopathy, multi-territory cerebral infarction, cytopenias, and a secondary hemophagocytic syndrome, due to a diffuse occlusive small-vessel process — most consistent with intravascular large B-cell lymphoma. --- ## Dr. Helena Brandt's Diagnosis Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma, with secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. --- ## Pathological Discussion **Dr. Sofia Nakamura (Pathology):** The diagnostic procedure was a set of random skin biopsies.
